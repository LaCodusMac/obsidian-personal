**You:** Are there any original trees on the east coast

**ChatGPT:** **Yes—there are still trees on the East Coast that were alive before European settlement**, along with small areas of forest that were never commercially logged. But they are rare and usually found on steep slopes, remote mountains, swamps, or land protected early enough to escape logging.

A useful distinction:

- **Native trees** are species naturally from the region. These are still widespread, although most individual trees are relatively young.
- **Old-growth forest** has developed for a long time with limited major human disturbance.
- **“Virgin” or primary forest** means there is no evidence it was ever logged. Truly untouched forest is much rarer.

Some notable survivors include:

- **Great Smoky Mountains, Tennessee/North Carolina:** Roughly 25% of the park’s forest is classified as old growth. Certain groves contain huge tulip poplars, hemlocks, magnolias, beech, buckeye, and other trees that predate the United States. citeturn474756search4turn474756search5
- **Adirondacks, New York:** Large remote sections escaped intensive clearing and contain some of the most extensive old-growth forest remaining in the eastern United States.
- **Big Reed Forest Preserve, Maine:** One of New England’s best examples of a relatively undisturbed northern forest.
- **Mohawk Trail State Forest, Massachusetts:** Small old-growth pockets contain eastern hemlocks dated to around **500 years old** and red spruce around **400 years old**. citeturn474756search1
- **New River Gorge, West Virginia:** The National Park Service identifies surviving old-growth patches, including forest dating to the 1600s. citeturn474756search20turn474756search25

What you generally will **not** find is a massive, perfectly untouched forest covering hundreds of miles like the East Coast had before widespread European settlement. Farming, shipbuilding, charcoal production, railroads, fires, and industrial logging cleared or altered nearly all of it. In New England, genuine old-growth forest now occupies **less than 1% of the landscape**. citeturn474756search23

So you can absolutely stand beside an East Coast tree that was growing in the 1500s or possibly earlier—but it will usually be an isolated survivor or part of a comparatively small protected pocket, not an endless primeval forest.
